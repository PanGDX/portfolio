### TLDR;

- Developed an autonomous maze-solving robot in a team of three.
- Implemented PID control for precise movement and navigation.
- Utilized custom-built IR sensors and an ultrasonic sensor for wall detection and distance measurement.
- A calibrated color sensor detected ground lines to trigger specific maneuvers like turns and rotations.

### Problem Statement
I needed to program and equip a robot using the baseline mBot with the necessary components to navigate a complex maze. I am not allowed to touch the walls. Upon detecting a distinct color like black or purple on the floor, the robot must perform a preprogrammed turn (90 degrees, 180, etc).

### Solution
To transform the baseline mBot into an autonomous maze-solving machine, we developed a  control system and sensor suite utilizing Arduino C++. The solution focused on three core pillars: precise locomotion, hardware-level sensor integration, and mathematical color classification.

1. Locomotion and Navigation via PID Control

The primary challenge of maze navigation is ensuring the robot moves in a straight line without hitting the walls. To achieve this, I implemented a Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) control loop. By constantly calculating an "error" value—the difference between the intended path and the actual position—and applying corrective power to the motors, the robot was able to maintain its trajectory without drifting into walls. This control theory was essential for smoothing out the jerky movements typical of basic motor commands, allowing for fluid navigation.

2. Hardware Engineering and IR Calibration

I moved beyond standard "plug-and-play" modules by integrating custom-built infrared (IR) sensors onto a breadboard interface. This required a deep dive into circuit configuration and signal processing. I calibrated these sensors to map raw analog voltage readings to accurate distance measurements. By fine-tuning the sensitivity thresholds, I ensured the robot could detect obstacles and maintain a consistent distance from walls with high-fidelity accuracy, mitigating the risk of physical contact.

3. Advanced Reflective Color Sensing and Calibration

The most complex hurdle was the color detection system. We were provided with a sensor that measured light intensity rather than chromaticity, paired with individual Red, Green, and Blue LEDs. To solve this, I developed a sequential flashing calibration routine:

    Data Acquisition: The robot would flash each LED individually and record the intensity of the reflected light.

    Normalization: Since different colors reflect different wavelengths with varying efficiency, I created a profile for each target color (Red, Green, Blue, Pink, Orange, Black) based on these reflected values.

    3D Euclidean Distance Logic: To distinguish between "tight" color clusters—such as the subtle differences between red, pink, and orange—I treated each color profile as a coordinate in a 3D RGB vector space. By calculating the 3D Euclidean distance between the real-time sensor reading and our calibrated benchmarks, the robot could mathematically determine the "nearest" color with high confidence.

4. Execution Logic

Finally, I synthesized these inputs into a robust state-machine logic. Upon reaching a color threshold, the 3D Euclidean algorithm would classify the floor tile and trigger the corresponding interrupt—such as a 90-degree pivot, a 180-degree U-turn, or a full stop. This combination of hardware calibration and geometric mathematics allowed the robot to navigate the maze with a level of autonomy and reliability that exceeded simple inequality-based logic.
